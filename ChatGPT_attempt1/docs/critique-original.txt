Add Laminar Flow Assumptions and Reynolds Number BoundsCategory: 1. Physical Accuracy & AssumptionsPlan Reference: Physics and Teaching Experience ("Assume constant density and viscosity, no slip, no imposed pressure gradient, and negligible edge effects") and Step 3: Free exploration.Why It Matters: The 1D diffusion equation $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}$ only holds for laminar, unidirectional flow between horizontal plates (or negligible gravitational body force along the flow axis). Plane Couette flow transitions to turbulence when the Reynolds number $Re = \frac{\rho U H}{\mu} = \frac{U H}{\nu}$ exceeds roughly $360\text{ to }1500$. If "Free exploration" allows arbitrary combinations of $\rho, \mu, H,$ and $U$, students will easily dial in high-$Re$ regimes where the laminar profile and diffusion timescale are physically invalid.Concrete Improvement: Add "laminar, unidirectional flow" to the assumptions list. Define explicit min/max numerical ranges for all sliders in Step 3, display the calculated Reynolds number $Re = UH/\nu$, and either constrain the sliders to the laminar regime or display a clear warning banner when $Re$ exceeds $1000$.E2. Track Moving-Wall Shear Stress to Answer the "Two Viscosities" QuestionCategory: 1. Physical Accuracy & 2. Teaching ClarityPlan Reference: Summary ("why do we need two definitions of viscosity?"), Step 2: Predict and observe ("changing both proportionally"), and Physics and Teaching Experience ("while steady shear stress is $\mu U/H$").Why It Matters: When a student doubles both $\mu$ and $\rho$ in Step 2, kinematic viscosity $\nu = \mu/\rho$ stays constant, so the velocity profile $u(y,t)$ evolves identically. If the plot only shows velocity and only mentions steady-state shear stress in text, a beginner may conclude that only $\nu$ matters and $\mu$ is redundant. Conversely, showing that the wall shear stress required to pull the upper plate doubles when $\mu$ and $\rho$ both double—and showing that transient acceleration stops at steady state because shear stress becomes uniform across the gap ($\tau_{\text{top}} = \tau_{\text{bottom}} = \mu U/H$)—directly resolves the core question of the lab.Concrete Improvement: Require the comparison dashboard in Steps 2 and 3 to display a dynamic metric or readout for the moving-plate shear stress (for $t \gt 0$) and the steady-state shear stress ($\mu U / H$). Explicitly state the pedagogical takeaway in the spec: $\nu$ governs how fast motion spreads through the fluid's mass, whereas $\mu$ governs how much shear stress (force per area) is required to drive that motion.E3. Introduce Physical Force Intuition Before the PDE, and Unify Steps 2–4Category: 2. Teaching Clarity & Scope for a BeginnerPlan Reference: Physics and Teaching Experience (placing $\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nu \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}$ at the top, Step 4: Explanation, and Step 5: Optional derivation).Why It Matters: Your target audience understands $\tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy}$ (slope of the velocity line) but has never studied PDEs or $\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}$ (curvature). Presenting the second-order PDE upfront without physical scaffolding is intimidating. Meanwhile, the qualitative heart of Step 5—that a thin fluid layer accelerates because the faster fluid above pulls it forward harder than the slower fluid below holds it back ($\tau_{\text{top}} \gt \tau_{\text{bottom}}$), resisted by the layer's mass ($\rho$)—is the exact bridge beginners need before predicting outcomes in Step 2. Additionally, listing the velocity plot, motion arrows, and time slider as "Step 4: Explanation" after Steps 2 and 3 makes the UI flow ambiguous: AI models often interpret this literally and build Steps 2 and 3 without visual plots or duplicate the plots three times.Concrete Improvement:In Step 1 (Overview), explain qualitatively how an imbalance in shear stress across a fluid layer causes acceleration ($F_{\text{net}} = ma$), and introduce the PDE alongside a plain-English translation of each term (Rate of velocity change = Kinematic viscosity $\times$ Curvature of the velocity profile). Bound the Early-Time Validation Window and Specify the Analytical SeriesCategory: 3. Numerical Method, Stability, and ValidationPlan Reference: Implementation and Validation ("Agreement with an independently evaluated analytical transient solution at positive times, targeting maximum velocity error below 1% of $U$").Why It Matters: Because the upper plate starts instantaneously at $t = 0$, the exact velocity profile at very small positive times ($t \to 0^+$) has a boundary layer of thickness $\delta \sim \sqrt{\nu t}$. Until $\delta$ grows across several spatial grid cells $\Delta y$, every uniform finite-difference grid will have a maximum pointwise error well above $1\%$ of $U$ at the node adjacent to the moving wall (often $5\%\text{ to }15\%$ during the first few time steps). Furthermore, if an AI model evaluates the analytical solution using a truncated Fourier sine series with too few terms (e.g., 10 terms), Gibbs oscillations near $t = 0^+$ will corrupt the analytical benchmark itself.Concrete Improvement:Write the exact analytical transient formula into the specification:$$u(y,t) = U\frac{y}{H} - \frac{2U}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{M} \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} \exp\left(-\frac{n^2 \pi^2 \nu t}{H^2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi y}{H}\right)$$with at least $M = 200$ terms.Specify that the maximum velocity error target ($\max_y \vert{}u_{\text{num}} - u_{\text{exact}}\vert{} / U \lt 0.01$) is evaluated for dimensionless times $t^* = \frac{\nu t}{H^2} \ge 0.01$ on a spatial grid of at least $N_y = 51$ nodes.E5. Prevent Explicit Time-Step Explosion Under Free ExplorationCategory: 3. Numerical Method & StabilityPlan Reference: Implementation and Validation ("Use a finite-difference thin-layer simulation with positive physical inputs and a time step satisfying the explicit diffusion stability condition") and Step 3: Free exploration.Why It Matters: For the explicit Forward-Time Central-Space (FTCS) scheme, stability requires Fourier mesh number $Fo = \frac{\nu \Delta t}{(\Delta y)^2} \le 0.5$. Because $\Delta t \propto \frac{H^2}{\nu N_y^2}$, if a student sets a small plate gap $H$ and high kinematic viscosity $\nu$ while exploring a fixed physical time window, $\Delta t$ shrinks by orders of magnitude. An unconstrained solver can easily attempt $10^6+$ time steps on a slider drag, locking up the Streamlit app.Concrete Improvement: Specify a fixed spatial grid (e.g., $N_y = 51$ points) and a fixed stability factor (e.g., $Fo = 0.4$, so $\Delta t = 0.4 \frac{(\Delta y)^2}{\nu}$). Scale the single-fluid simulation's default maximum time horizon to $t_{\max} = 0.6 \frac{H^2}{\nu}$ (since the $1\%$ settling time is $t_{\text{settle}} \approx 0.421 \frac{H^2}{\nu}$), which guarantees the simulation always completes in roughly $1,500$ time steps regardless of slider values. For side-by-side comparisons in Step 2, set the shared physical time horizon to the larger of the two fluids' settling times and cap the maximum total time steps (or raise $N_y$ / sub-step efficiently) so both runs remain instantaneous.E6.
